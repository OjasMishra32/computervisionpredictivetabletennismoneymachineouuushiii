\begin{csabstract}
We built a predictive ball-tracking engine that calls a point (ball out, or into the net) before the ball lands. On
held-out real table-tennis video our live causal engine made its calls a median 162\,ms before the
ball reached the table end, none wrong but few (4 of 41 misses), in real time at
119.9\,fps; a frame becomes a ready order in 54\,ms. On real broadcast tennis (one TV
camera, a pretrained ball detector, held-out games) its out calls were right 5 of 5 at the bounce and 3 of 3 when
33\,ms ahead. Speed is worth money because, in real Polymarket data on 13,084 ATP and WTA matches
(1,310 at Grand Slams), whoever learns the point first sets the price: wallets trading within 3\,s of a
point earn after fees in all 11 months, and each second of feed delay costs a simulated
computer-vision (CV) trader \$42–74 a day. Copying those wallets at their own fills has a Sharpe
ratio of 14.5 in sample and 6.7 out of sample, which doubled fees erase. At an assumed
1\,s feed our CV trader makes +\$4 a day out of sample pre-registered and
+\$57 (Sharpe 8.8) post hoc. Every blind test of a book we could trade
failed.

\vspace{3pt}\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G14, G12, C58.\enspace \textbf{Keywords:} computer vision; ball tracking; prediction
markets; latency arbitrage; in-play betting; tennis.
\end{csabstract}

% ======================================================================================== 1 Introduction and summary
\section{Introduction and summary}\label{sec:summary}
A camera films the court; our model finds the ball in every frame, fits its flight, predicts where it will land and
calls the point before the bounce. We ask what that head start is worth: a Polymarket tennis price \emph{is} the chance
that a player wins, and quotes lag each point by about a second, so whoever learns it first sets the price. We
contribute:
\begin{keybox}
\item \textbf{Predictive ball tracking}, causal and real time (119.9\,fps, 4.6\,ms a
frame on one GPU), tested on real held-out table tennis and broadcast tennis (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A wallet-level map of who is fast}: the wallets that trade first earn in all 11
months and everyone else loses; copying them at their fills (\emph{v2}) has a Sharpe ratio of 14.5 IS,
6.7 OOS.
\item \textbf{A dollar price for each second of feed delay} from the latency identity of Eq.~\ref{eq:budget}:
\$42–74 a day for the first second. With a 1\,s feed our order executes 2,119\,ms
after the point, inside the 3\,s bar. Every blind test of a book we could trade failed.
\end{keybox}

\section{Economic hypothesis}\label{sec:hyp}
A Markov model of points, games and sets gives the exact chance that each player wins from any score
\citep{klaassen2001}, and a Polymarket price is that chance in cents, so each point moves fair value by a known amount
at a known moment: the \emph{swing} \(\Delta(k)\), the match-win chance if point \(k\) is won minus that if it is lost,
averages 5.7\% and reaches 38.4\% on a big point (Appendix~A).

The news reaches traders in tiers: the court first, then video, then public score feeds
28.2--44.1\,s after the price has moved (Fig.~\ref{fig:race}). A maker whose quote still
rests after the point is picked off by whoever knows first: the adverse selection of \citet{glosten1985} and the latency
race of \citet{budish2015} and \citet{aquilina2022}; in-play betting prices react late, and courtsiders trade on it
\citep{croxson2014,brown2012}. We see them: on 482 points recorded live, a median
\$222 of quotes still rested at stale prices 0.25\,s before the reprice, and
\$0 half a second after it. Polymarket holds each \emph{taker} order (one that hits a resting quote) for
1\,s so makers can cancel (a venue setting). The edge is shrinking: the fast tier
grew from 4 to 131 wallets as its edge fell 0.20¢ a
month (\(t = −3.98\)); at that pace v2's OOS +0.60¢ reaches our halving trigger
in 1.5 months and zero in 3.0.

\begin{hypbox}
\textbf{H6 (pre-registered).} We expect wallets that trade within 3\,s of a score move in Polymarket ATP and WTA match
markets to earn a positive net \emph{markout} over 30\,s (profit at the price 30\,s later, after fees), because the
other side is a maker who has not yet cancelled a stale quote. The edge persists because physics and data licences
decide who learns first. If so, fast wallets picked on past months stay positive every month and out of sample; H6
fails if the markout is \(\leq 0\) in over a third of months, or out of sample. H1--H4 were committed before any result
(\texttt{7232986}, 2026-10-03 09:50 UTC); H6 was written after the in-sample tier results
(D5) and frozen before we opened the held-out data.
\end{hypbox}

\section{Data and universe}\label{sec:data}
Of the table's 1,310 Grand Slam matches,
415 are at the Australian Open, 412 at Roland Garros and 483 at the
US Open (928 main draw); Wimbledon is a separate Polymarket series outside our universe. The CV trader
works in the 7,755 matches from May 15 on, when the hold was 1\,s
(895 of them at Slams), trading in about 681 IS and
246 OOS per seed. The universe: every resolved ATP/WTA singles match market with at least
\$5,000 traded (\$2.84B in all). \emph{In sample} (IS) is the first 80\% of matches by
start time; \emph{out of sample} (OOS) is the last 2,617, from 2026-08-25 14:15 UTC. We looked at the
OOS while building v2 and the CV simulation: it is \emph{burned}. For a blind test, which also checks survivorship,
we held back 11,307 smaller markets and opened them once per frozen rule. Six tapes hit the API's offset
cap, 2,889 early matches paid no fee, 2.89\% of markets settled 50/50 (kept), tape
timestamps run 1.98\,s late, no corporate actions apply. Timing: ESPN and the WTA
\citep{espn,wtaapi}.

% ======================================================================================== 4 Methodology
\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\para{The fast tier and v2} We flag a score move when the 10\,s volume-weighted price moves at least
4¢ away from its prior 60\,s. Each month a wallet joins the fast tier if, in earlier months, it
traded at least 30 times 0--3\,s after a flag, in at least 10
matches, with \(t > 3\). v2 copies their trades, sizes each by its resolution risk (the best of
55 sizing rules, picked walk-forward), caps orders at \$1,000 and exposure at
100 shares net per match, and holds to resolution. At their own prices it measures what their speed
is worth, not what we could earn: copying the same trades 3\,s later loses (Fig.~\ref{fig:edge}a).

\para{Being fast ourselves} Our second strategy: a computer-vision (CV) model watches the video, calls the point
early and sends a taker order before the stale quote is cancelled. We have no video of the matches we trade,
so we simulate this trader on past points with an assumed feed latency (licensed feed not purchased); all other
parameters are measured. The order wins only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budget}
  V + \delta + D - \ell \;<\; L + R \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V \;<\; L + R - D - \delta + \ell ,
\end{equation}
where \(V\) is the feed delay (how late the video reaches us), \(\delta\) our pipeline plus network time, \(D\) the
1\,s taker hold, \(\ell\) how early the CV calls the point, \(L\) the \emph{stamp lag} (how long
after the ball lands the official point stamp is logged) and \(R\) when the book reprices relative to that
stamp. On 482 points the book moved 1.2\,s before the stamp. Nobody
publishes \(L\), so we show two readings: 2.0\,s, which we \emph{pre-registered} (written before any
result; \texttt{PREREG.md}, 17:25 UTC), and 3.14\,s, a \emph{post
hoc} estimate from one day of data. The simulated trades are past points where the price later moved \(\geq\)4¢:
\emph{selected on outcomes, not ex ante}.

\para{Our vision on real video} On the TrackNet broadcast set we built court calibration, a 3D flight fit and landing
predictors, and tested them once on games 8--10 (132 bounces). From one TV camera, on the
pretrained detector's own track, the landing point is 17\,cm off at the bounce and
0.7–1.2\,m off when predicted 33--300\,ms ahead (median). With thresholds set on games 1--7, out calls
were right 5 of 5 at the bounce (half the outs), 3 of 3 at 33\,ms and
3 of 4 at 67\,ms ahead, and fail from 100\,ms (2 of 5). Only the bounce-time call met our
95\% precision rule on games 1--7, and the detector weights \citep{yastrebksv}, not ours, saw frames of all ten games,
so these are in-distribution numbers. On simulated Hawk-Eye-class physics our spin-aware model cuts the landing
spread 10-fold at 200\,ms (out-call precision 1, recall
0.98). On held-out table tennis at 120\,fps our live causal engine called
4 of 41 misses early (median lead 162\,ms), none wrongly, at
119.9\,fps on one L4 GPU; outside play it makes about 30 phantom calls an hour,
and a rally gate (trade a miss call only within 2.0\,s of a bounce call, set before the test) removes
5 of 7 calls on unlabelled balls in its held-out log but keeps only
3 of 5 correct calls and leaves about 8 an hour:
not yet safe to trade. With a simulated 1\,s feed, our 54\,ms pipeline,
65\,ms of network and the 1\,s hold, an order can execute 2,119\,ms after the point
(p99 2,218\,ms; 24 runs, order built, not sent), inside the organizers' 3\,s bar
(Fig.~\ref{fig:f2t}).

\para{Costs and validation} Takers pay \(f(q) = r\,q\,(1-q)\) a share at price \(q\), or \(10{,}000\,r\,(1-q)\) bps of
notional; today \(r = 5\%\), so a trade at 50¢ pays 1.25¢. Each match pays its own
fee and every trade the spread it crossed; we also double both. v2 paid 120 and
179\,bps in fees (IS, OOS) and still netted 273 and
114\,bps. We tune only in sample, walk-forward (fit on past months, test on the next).

\para{A Sharpe ratio of 14.5 is a red flag} We assumed a bug and found one: the detector's start

95\% CI above zero in sample (Sharpe 4.1–16.8), the v2-safe grid's probability of backtest overfitting
is 9–24\% across block choices (A6), and across all 14 feed delays from
0 to 60\,s the CV profit never rises by more than \$0.09 a
day (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed}).

\para{What speed is worth} Pre-registered, the CV trader breaks even at a 1.01\,s feed delay
out of sample (Table~\ref{tab:lat}; curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:speed}): the call must reach the venue
0.89\,s before the umpire's stamp. Post hoc it makes +\$57 a day at
1\,s (+\$8 at the low end of the stamp-lag interval), but read per point the post hoc inference
loses (−\$17 a day). Every reading loses at 3\,s, so meeting the 3\,s bar is not enough. A replay
of 9 matches recorded live on Oct~3 against their real books, calling every point ex ante, loses in
36 of 36 settings (−0.94¢ a share at 1\,s;
Fig.~\ref{fig:replay}): the gap is knowing which points matter in advance.

\para{Blind tests} On 11,307 markets we had never examined (10,855 ITF; pre-registered at
\texttt{337bf7c} before fetching any tape), frozen v2 earned +2.02¢
[1.14, 2.93] a share IS but +1.22¢ [−0.19, 2.65] OOS, so it fails. Pre-registered secondary statistics show the mechanism holding blind: the fast tier beat the rest

% ======================================================================================== 6 Risk
\section{Risk management}\label{sec:risk}
\para{Limits and de-risking} Each order is at most \$1,000 and each match at most
100 shares net at 5--95¢, which caps the open position at \$95, not the loss:
buying both sides of a match can lock in more (worst match −\$206 IS,
−\$154 OOS), so a \$1,000 daily loss ends the day. Set in advance, we stop if
the score feed is over 2\,s stale, vision over 1\,s stale, or
latency above its 95th percentile; we halve size if the trailing 30-day edge falls below 0.3¢,
and stop if it reaches zero, if drawdown hits 5\%, or if the venue changes its fee or hold. None
fired in sample (trailing edge \(\geq\)+0.74¢, worst day −\$551).

\para{Model, concentration and tail risk} Wrong CV calls always fill, so an out call trades only at confidence
\(\geq\)0.95 and one confirmed false call halts the day; 2.5 phantom calls a day would erase the
pre-registered 1\,s OOS P\&L (32 post hoc). The top five copied wallets carry
81\% of v2's P\&L in sample and 142\% out of sample; without them v2 earns
−0.34¢ [−1.20, 0.46] OOS. A per-wallet cap (\$1,000 a day, and a wallet
whose trailing edge turns negative is retired), chosen in sample from 12 more variants and pre-registered at \texttt{4a5a19d},
ran once on the burned OOS (non-blind): +0.91¢ [0.20, 1.66], Sharpe 7.6,
worst day −\$284 instead of −\$469; it costs 37\% of
in-sample dollars and the top five still carry 129\%. The UMA oracle settles markets \citep{pmuma},
US persons may only close positions \citep{pmgeoblock}, and courtsiding breaches ticket terms \citep{itf2026}. Factor
risk is nil (largest factor \(|t| = 1.36\); \(\alpha\) has \(t = 8.48\))
\citep{fama1993,carhart1997}.

% ======================================================================================== 7 Liquidity
\section{Liquidity and capacity}\label{sec:liq}
\para{How much capital} v2: \$22,754, the largest size (1\(\times\)) at which its OOS CI stays above
zero (5\(\times\) loses); it trades \$7,182 a day, 0.07\% of match volume. The
CV book: \$40,000--\$73,000 post hoc (OOS--IS), where its Sharpe halves, and at most
\$177 a day (on \$83,000 OOS; Fig.~\ref{fig:capacity}); pre-registered, none
(Sharpe 2.7 IS, 0.8 OOS at its smallest size). It takes at most half the
stale depth at each point: 0.22\% of in-play volume but 9\% of what the
fast tier trades 0--3\,s after a point. These are upper bounds: we model no price impact beyond the stale depth, and up
to 131 fast wallets compete for the same quotes. At 1\,s the book could pay at most
\$1,644 a month for data post hoc and \$55 pre-registered, against feed
prices of \$1,250--\$10,000. At today's fee COURTSIDE prices speed; it is not yet a
business.

% ======================================================================================== 8 Limitations
\section{Limitations and next steps}\label{sec:limits}
The stamp lag \(L\) is unmeasured and moves the break-even one for one (Eq.~\ref{eq:budget}); the 1\,s hold dominates
it, since even for a 20\,ms bot the inferred lag is 2.85\,s. One session with a licensed feed and a few
test orders would measure \(L\); at 1\,s the feed pays for itself only if \(L \geq 3.0\)\,s, a purchase
we have not made. Our out calls are tested on real broadcast tennis and table tennis, never on
the matches we trade; trading is simulated and we never sent an order. A fee rise, a shorter hold or more fast wallets
would break it.\label{lastmain}

\csappendix
\section*{Appendix A\quad Calculations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix}
Everything needed for scoring is in the main pages. Below: formulas with worked examples (A), every test (B), extra
figures (C) and the records (D). Each value is in \texttt{results/paper/numbers.json} with its source file.

\para{A1. Sharpe ratio} \(\text{SR} = \sqrt{365}\,\bar d / s_d\), where \(d_t\) is net P\&L on every calendar day
(zero on days without trades) and \(s_d\) its standard deviation. v2 IS: \(\bar d = \$196.24\),
\(s_d = \$258.87\) over \(T = 206\) days, so the daily ratio is 0.758
and \(\text{SR} = 0.758 \times 19.105 = 14.48\). OOS:
\(\$92.19 / \$264.15 \times 19.105 = 6.67\).

\para{A2. Sortino ratio} \(\sqrt{365}\,\bar d / \text{DD}\), where the downside deviation is \(\text{DD} =
\sqrt{\tfrac1T\sum_t \min(d_t,0)^2}\). For v2 OOS, \(\text{DD} = \$125.64\), so the ratio is
\(\sqrt{365}\times \$92.19 / \$125.64 = 14.0\).

\para{A3. Maximum drawdown} \(\text{MDD} = \min_t \big(E_t - \max_{s\le t} E_s\big)\), where \(E_t\) is cumulative
P\&L, as a share of capital. v2 OOS: \(−\$469 / \$22,754 = −2.06\%\).

\para{A4. Probabilistic Sharpe} The chance that the true daily Sharpe beats \(\text{SR}^*\), given the sample length
\(T\), skew \(\gamma_3\) and kurtosis \(\gamma_4\) \citep{bailey2014}:
\[\text{PSR}(\text{SR}^*) = \Phi\Big((\widehat{\text{SR}}-\text{SR}^*)\sqrt{T-1}\,\Big/\sqrt{1-\gamma_3\widehat{\text{SR}}+\tfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}\widehat{\text{SR}}^2}\Big).\]
v2 OOS: \(\widehat{\text{SR}} = 0.349\), \(T = 40\),
\(\gamma_3 = 0.326\), \(\gamma_4 = 3.26\), so \(\text{PSR}(0) = 0.987\).

\para{A5. Deflated Sharpe} \(\text{DSR} = \text{PSR}(\text{SR}_0)\), where \(\text{SR}_0\) is the best Sharpe we would
expect from \(N\) tries on pure noise, with \(\gamma\) the Euler--Mascheroni constant:
\[\text{SR}_0 = \sqrt{V}\Big((1-\gamma)\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1-\tfrac1N\big) + \gamma\,\Phi^{-1}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{Ne}\big)\Big).\] v2 OOS with \(N =
3,386\) and \(V = 1/(T-1)\): the two \(\Phi^{-1}\) terms are 3.436 and 3.698,
\(\text{SR}_0 = 0.574\) a day (10.97 a year), and DSR \(= 0.075\).
Table~\ref{tab:head} shows the lowest DSR over three choices of \(V\).

\para{A6. Probability of backtest overfitting} \citep{bailey2017}. Split the daily P\&L of 24
variants into \(S = 16\) blocks. For each of the \(\binom{16}{8} = 12,870\) ways
to call half the blocks in sample, take the best in-sample variant, find its rank \(\omega\) out of sample and set
\(\lambda = \ln\frac{\omega}{1-\omega}\); PBO \(= \Pr(\lambda \le 0)\). v2-safe grid: PBO \(= 15\%\)
(median \(\lambda = 1.27\)).

\para{A7. Stationary block bootstrap} \citep{politis1994}. Resample days in blocks of random length (mean 5 days)
10,000 times and read the percentiles. v2 OOS Sharpe: [1.9, 12.3], standard error 2.65,
\(P(\text{SR}\le 0) = 0.0023\).

\para{A8. Factor regression} \(r_t = \alpha + \beta_M\,\text{MktRF}_t + \beta_S\,\text{SMB}_t + \beta_H\,\text{HML}_t
+ \beta_U\,\text{Mom}_t + \varepsilon_t\) on daily return on capital, with Newey--West errors (5 lags)
\citep{fama1993,carhart1997,newey1987,french}. v2 IS (138 days): \(\alpha = 0.801\%\) a day
(\(t = 8.48\)); \(\beta_M = 0.173\) (\(t = 1.36\)),
\(\beta_S = 0.085\), \(\beta_H = 0.044\), \(\beta_U = 0.028\);
\(R^2 = 3.3\%\).

\para{A9. Taker fee} \(f(q) = r\,q(1-q)\) a share at price \(q\), or \(r(1-q)\) per dollar, which is
\(10{,}000\,r(1-q)\) bps. At \(r = 5\%\): \(q = 0.5\) pays 1.25¢
(250\,bps) and \(q = 0.8\) pays 0.80¢ (100\,bps).

\para{A10. Point swing} \(\Delta(k)\) of Section~\ref{sec:hyp}, with the match-win probability computed exactly by recursion over points,
games, tiebreaks and sets (\texttt{src/markov.py}). Two equal players who win 0.645 of service points:
at the first point \(\Pr(\text{win}) = 50.0\%\), moving to 51.2\% or
47.8\%, so \(\Delta = 3.4\%\). Serving at 5--5, 30--40 in the deciding
set, \(\Pr(\text{win}) = 33.7\%\), moving to 47.3\% or 8.9\%, so
\(\Delta = 38.4\%\). The mean \(|\Delta|\) over a match is 5.7\%.

\para{A11. Latency budget} Eq.~\ref{eq:budget}. With the pre-registered \(L = 2.0\)\,s, \(D =
1\)\,s and our measured \(\delta = 0.119\)\,s, a point whose book reprices at the
median \(R = −1.16\)\,s needs \(V < −0.28 + \ell\): no feed wins it
without an early call. At the 90th percentile, \(R = 0.08\)\,s and \(V < 0.96\)\,s.
The sweep's break-even (1.09\,s IS) comes from these late reprices.

\para{A12. Capital and return on capital} Capital is three times the peak dollars locked in open positions,
each held 4\,h or until resolution. v2 IS: peak \$9,434, capital \$28,302; return \(= 365\,\bar
d/\text{capital} = 365 \times \$196.24 / \$28,302 = 253\%\) a year.

\para{A13. Capacity and volume share} Capacity is the capital at which the seed-mean Sharpe falls to half its
smallest-size value; share is our notional a day over market volume a day. CV, post hoc, IS, just below half Sharpe:
\$10,428 a day against \$4.71M of in-play volume (0.22\%) and
\$117,047 traded by the fast tier 0--3\,s after a point (9\%).
