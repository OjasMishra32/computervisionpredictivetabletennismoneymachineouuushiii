RISK PREMIUM
You are paid for holding a risk that other investors would rather not hold.
Investors who pay to shed the risk: hedgers, insurance buyers, holders who can’t stomach drawdowns.
Value and momentum premia show up across stocks, bonds, currencies and commodities.
Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, Value and Momentum Everywhere (2013); Ilmanen, Expected Returns (2011)
The risk arrives all at once, or the trade gets crowded and the premium shrinks.
leverage-constrained investors overpay for safe-looking assets
BEHAVIORAL BIAS
Other traders make systematic mistakes, and those mistakes leave patterns in prices.
Investors who underreact to news, then chase the move late, or who anchor on old prices.
Time-series momentum: an asset’s own past 12-month return predicts its next month across futures markets.
Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, Time Series Momentum (2012)
The bias is arbitraged away, or the pattern reverses sharply in a crash.
nearby lookbacks fail (a lone spike, not a plateau)
the out-of-sample Sharpe is near zero
STRUCTURAL CONSTRAINT
Rules, mandates or institutional habits force some players to trade regardless of price.
Index funds rebalancing on a set date, funds that must sell downgraded bonds, products rolling futures on a schedule.
Predictable, mandated flows such as index rebalances and futures rolls.
The rule changes, or enough traders front-run the flow that its price impact disappears.
LIQUIDITY PROVISION
You are paid for supplying immediacy: taking the other side when someone needs to trade now.
Hurried sellers and buyers who pay you, through the price, to trade right now.
Short-term reversal: buying what was sold hard yesterday and selling what was bought hard.
Costs eat the edge, or the hurried trader knew something you didn’t.
stronger reversals after larger, high-volume moves

P-HACKING
DATA SNOOPING
Testing dozens of signals, lookbacks or thresholds and reporting only the best one.
Write the hypothesis first and report how many variants you tried. Show that nearby parameter values also work; a broad plateau is far more convincing than one sharp peak.
OVERFITTING
TOO MANY PARAMETERS
Many free parameters, complex rules, or ML models tuned on a short history.
Keep the parameter count low and favor simple rules with an economic reason behind them. Use walk-forward or cross-validation that respects time order.
USING FUTURE INFORMATION
Using information not available at decision time: same-bar close for signal and fill, revised economic data, future index membership.
Lag every signal at least one bar. Trade at the next open or close. Use point-in-time data when it exists.
SURVIVORSHIP BIAS
ONLY TODAY’S SURVIVORS
Backtesting only on today’s index members or tickers that still trade.
Use a point-in-time universe, or state the bias clearly and estimate its effect.
IGNORING COSTS
GROSS RETURNS ONLY
Reporting gross returns, or assuming a high-turnover strategy trades for free.
Take commissions, spread and slippage out of every number, and show what happens when costs double.
LEAKING THE TEST SET
TUNING ON THE TEST SET
Looking at out-of-sample results, then going back and changing the strategy.
Don’t touch the out-of-sample period until the end, then evaluate it once.
MISLEADING SHARPE
SHARPE WITHOUT CONTEXT
Sharpe from too little data, overlapping returns, or a strategy short volatility that hides tail risk.
Report max drawdown, skew and worst month next to Sharpe. If you get a Sharpe above 3 on daily data, assume something is wrong until you find out what.
REGIME DEPENDENCE
ONE GOOD PERIOD
All the profit comes from one period, such as 2020 or 2022.
Break results down by year or regime and talk about it in the note.
UNREALISTIC CAPACITY
IGNORING MARKET IMPACT
Trading illiquid names at full size, or ignoring market impact.
Size positions as a fraction of average daily volume and estimate capacity in dollars.
LIMITS

DE-RISKING

WHAT JUDGES WANT TO SEE
KEY TERMS
Returns alone don’t win anything. Judges would rather see a Sharpe of 0.6 you can defend than a 4 you can’t explain.
Who’s on the other side of your trade?
Every trade has a counterparty. Before you look at any results, write down who’s taking the other side and why the opportunity hasn’t gone away. That’s the core of your Economic Foundation score, and having it in writing makes it harder to fool yourself once backtests start coming in.
COMMIT IT FIRST
Commit your hypothesis to the repo before your first backtest. The commit timestamp shows judges it came before the results.
Get clean data, then hold some back
Pick a liquid, publicly traded market you can get clean data for. Registered teams get sponsor data from Databento and Webull, and free public sources are fine too. Once you have your history, set aside the most recent part and leave it alone until the end.

Try to break your own backtest
Below is a small but complete backtest: a trend-following rule on twelve years of synthetic prices. Change the lookback, raise costs, turn on the lookahead bug, and when you’re done tuning, evaluate the out-of-sample period once to see if the edge holds.
LAG EVERY SIGNAL
If a signal uses today’s close, you can’t also trade at today’s close. Trade at the next open or close instead. Switch the lab to
SAME BAR
DOUBLE THE COSTS
Report everything net of commissions, spread and slippage, and show what happens when costs double. If the edge disappears, say so.
REPORT THE MINIMUM
For in-sample and out-of-sample separately: annualized return, volatility, Sharpe, max drawdown, turnover, and an equity curve.
Common ways backtests go wrong
Judges see these every year. Each one makes a backtest look better without making the strategy any better. Catch them yourself and you’ll do better on Economic Foundation and Performance.
TRYING MANY VARIANTS ON NOISE
CHECK THE SIMPLE EXPLANATION
When a result looks good, look for a simpler reason first. Usually it’s a bug, a bias, or exposure to something well known like market beta, momentum or value.
What happens with real money
Two of the five judging criteria are about what comes after the backtest: how you control risk, and how much money the strategy could manage before trading costs and market impact eat the edge.
ESTIMATING CAPACITY
Writing the quant note
The quant note is a PDF of at most five pages, figures and tables included. References and an optional appendix of extra charts don’t count toward the limit, but judges aren’t required to read the appendix. Use 11pt font or larger and standard margins.
How it’s scored
Judges score five criteria from 1 to 10, for a total out of 50. Code isn’t scored on its own, but it supports your Performance score, and judges will spot-check that it runs and matches your note.
SCORE CAP
If judges can’t run your code, or it gives materially different numbers than your note, your Performance and Analytical Evidence score is capped at 4. The same cap applies if they find lookahead bias or tuning on the out-of-sample period.
How to submit
Submit two things on Devpost: your quant note as a PDF and a link to a public GitHub repo. Both are required. A note without code, or code without a note, won’t be judged.

THE CHAIN & HIDDEN SPOT
For each event, the chain as of t_pre. Spot is recovered by put-call parity, S = K·e^(−rT) + C − P, iterating to the at-the-money strike. Then pick the expiry per bucket, the strikes, and pull every leg’s bars once for its whole life.
THE P&L ENGINE
Five strategies from four legs, marked at entry and every exit, as P&L per $1 of stock at entry so a $60 and a $600 stock compare. The stock leg is the synthetic long.
SCOREBOARD & PLACEBO
Mean P&L per strategy and horizon with a 95% bootstrap interval, then the same measurement on ordinary days for the same names. The finding is the gap between the two. Also the yardstick: |realized| ÷ implied move.
OUT-OF-SAMPLE

A SUB-TRACK
Every Systematic Trading rule applies here too: a hypothesis stated before results, results net of costs, every data source cited, a quant note of at most five pages, and a public GitHub repo. Massive’s challenge adds the data, the strategy menu and the fixed horizons. One exception: the notebook’s own out-of-sample window and the judges’ sealed window replace the track’s 20% holdout rule.
READ THE TRACK RULES →
NOT P&L
You’re judged on the novelty of the category-to-strategy link, the rigor of the analysis, whether it replicates on a sealed window, the realism of the trade, and clarity. A well-argued null result with a clear decay curve beats a lucky backtest.
One number that matters
No options experience needed to start. Every strategy here is a bet on one relationship: the implied move the chain charged on the pre-event session against the move the stock actually made. Buying options wins when realized beats implied; selling wins when it doesn’t.

IMPLIED vs REALIZED
Pick a shape, defend the pairing
Five standard strategies, each explainable in two sentences and buildable from one option chain: OptionsPlaybook’s four Rookie strategies plus one Veteran, the long call. Finding and defending the link between a filing category and one of these is the challenge.

The long call teaches what a call costs. The covered call sells that same contract. The protective put buys the insurance instead of selling it, the collar is both at once, and the cash-secured put is the covered call’s payoff without owning the shares. More on each at

🐊 What is Gator Quant Hacks? 🟢
Gator Quant Hacks is a national quantitative finance competition bringing together undergraduate and graduate students for a weekend of research, mathematical problem solving, quantitative engineering, and systematic trading. Competitors participate in one of three parallel tracks, work through the weekend, attend sponsor and partner programming, and present or submit their work for judging.
The event is designed to bring together students across finance, economics, mathematics, statistics, computer science, and engineering. Programming throughout the weekend includes sponsor introductions, workshops, mixers, mini-events, meals, and competition time.
